Abstract 

Vocal behavior provides important signals for speech-based depression detection, but its in-terpretation often depends on what is being said and how it functions in context. However, existing methods typically treat acoustic cues as context-independent markers, making it dif-ficult to distinguish vocal form from its context-dependent communicative function. We pro-pose ParaCalib, a framework that semanti-cally calibrates paralinguistic behavior by in-terpreting vocal patterns relative to utterance-level semantic context and inferred commu-nicative function and representing them in a comparable state space. Concretely, Para-Calib uses an Audio-Language Model (ALM) to generate contextualized vocal descriptions and an LLM-based Paralinguistic State Extrac-tor (PSE) to map these descriptions into a struc-tured Semantically Calibrated Paralinguistic (SC-Para) representation. ParaCalib achieves the highest mean Macro-F1 among the eval-uated methods, reaching 71.9% on DAIC-WOZ and 90.5% on MODMA. Our controlled analysis provides direct evidence of semantic calibration at the PSE stage: under a fixed caption-level acoustic description, varying the accompanying semantic context changes the inferred depression-related paralinguistic evi-dence. Our exploratory analysis further iden-tifies recurring configurations of paralinguistic states associated with depression labels rather than a single uniformly dominant state. 

1 Introduction 

Depression is a prevalent mental disorder associ-ated with substantial disability and impaired daily functioning (World Health Organization, 2025). Early and scalable screening is central to compu-tational mental health research (Galatzer-Levy and Onnela, 2023). Speech is a promising modality for this purpose because it is non-invasive, relatively easy to collect during clinical interviews or remote

interactions, and psychologically informative, cap-turing both what a person says and how they say it (Cummins et al., 2015; Li et al., 2025b). Most speech-based depression detection meth-ods follow what we refer to as an acoustic-marker paradigm: they seek vocal patterns that are sta-tistically associated with depression and use them as participant-level indicators. Early approaches rely on low-level descriptors (LLDs), including prosodic, spectral, voice-quality, and temporal fea-tures, motivated by clinical links between depres-sion and psychomotor slowing, longer pauses, re-duced vocal energy, monotonic prosody, and al-tered voice quality (Ellgring and Scherer, 1996; Cummins et al., 2015; Yamamoto et al., 2020). More recent end-to-end and self-supervised (SSL) models learn representations from raw waveforms, time-frequency inputs, or large unlabeled speech corpora (Ma et al., 2016; Chen et al., 2022b; arXiv:2610.01103v1 [eess.AS] 1 Oct 2026 Baevski et al., 2020; Hsu et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2022a; Wu et al., 2023; Li et al., 2025a). Despite their architectural differences, these approaches generally map acoustic form directly to depression-related evidence, without explicitly modeling how utterance-level semantic context and inferred com-municative function shape the interpretation of vo-cal behavior. This mismatch arises for two reasons. First, de-pression is clinically heterogeneous: major depres-sive episodes may involve either psychomotor re-tardation or agitation, suggesting that depression-related speech need not follow a single mono-tonic acoustic profile (American Psychiatric Asso-ciation, 2022; Alpert et al., 2001). For example, depressive speech may be slower and more paused in some cases, but tense or accelerated in others. Second, the same acoustic form can serve differ-ent communicative functions. A long pause, flat-tened pitch contour, reduced intensity, or slowed speaking rate may reflect depressive psychomotor changes, but may also result from hesitation, em-phasis, syntactic planning, turn-taking, topic shifts, or the semantic content being expressed (Cole, 2015; Wagner and Watson, 2010). In short, de-pression can manifest through different vocal pat-terns, while the same vocal pattern can serve dif-ferent communicative functions. As illustrated in Figure 1, an acoustic pattern that appears strongly depression-related under the traditional paradigm may receive a different interpretation when con-sidered together with the utterance and its commu-nicative context. Motivated by this observation, we propose Para-Calib, 1 a framework for representing utterance-level vocal behavior as semantically calibrated par-alinguistic states. We define semantic calibration as interpreting vocal behavior relative to utterance-level semantic context and inferred communicative function and expressing the resulting interpretation in a structured, comparable state space. ParaCalib operationalizes this process by combining ALM-generated contextualized vocal descriptions with PSE mapping into predefined SC-Para dimensions. For each participant, the valid utterance-level SC-Para vectors form an instance bag, which is ag-gregated by an attention-based MIL classifier for participant-level prediction. Experiments on DAIC-WOZ and MODMA

Controlled analysis fur-ther shows that, under a fixed caption-level acous-tic description, the PSE-derived evidence changes with semantic context, providing direct evidence that semantic calibration is operationalized at the PSE stage. Exploratory analysis identifies specific combinations of states associated with depression labels. Together, these findings support structured, context-sensitive, and compositional modeling of vocal behavior for speech-based depression detec-tion. 

2 Related Work 

2.1 Depression Detection from Speech 

The AVEC challenges have established widely used benchmarks for computational depression as-sessment (Valstar et al., 2013; Ringeval et al., 2019). Early systems primarily relied on hand-crafted prosodic, spectral, temporal, and voice-quality descriptors (Li et al., 2025b). Their de-pendence on manually selected features motivated CNN-, RNN-, and Transformer-based models that learn representations directly from waveforms or time–frequency inputs (Chlasta et al., 2019; Dubagunta et al., 2019; Saidi et al., 2020; Oth-mani et al., 2021; Vázquez-Romero and Gallardo-Antolín, 2020; Wang et al., 2022; Salekin et al., 2018; Zhao et al., 2020; Muzammel et al., 2020). Pretrained speech encoders, including wav2vec 2.0 (Baevski et al., 2020), HuBERT (Hsu et al., 2021), WavLM (Chen et al., 2022a), and Whisper (Radford et al., 2023), further improve representation transfer in data-limited settings (Zhang et al., 2021; Toto et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2022b; Ravi et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2023; Li et al., 2025a; Zhang et al., 2025b). Despite their different architectures, these approaches generally use learned representations as generic features for participant-level prediction rather than explicitly modeling how vocal behavior should be interpreted in context. Several studies incorporate linguistic informa-tion or examine its potential confounding effects. Syntactic information has been used to guide acoustic feature extraction and multimodal fu-sion (Morales et al., 2018). These studies demonstrate that linguistic content can provide relevant contextual information while also introducing spurious label associations. How-ever, they do not explicitly represent how the mean-ing of a vocal pattern changes with utterance-level semantic context and inferred communicative func-tion. 

2.2 Audio-Language Models for Affective Speech Understanding 

Audio-Language Models (ALMs), such as SALMONN, Qwen-Audio, Qwen2-Audio, and GAMA, connect audio encoders with pretrained language models to support general audio un-derstanding (Tang et al., 2024; Chu et al., 2023, 2024; Ghosh et al., 2024). In affective speech, these models have been applied to closed-set emotion recognition, free-form vocal captioning, and prediction refinement grounded in transcripts and acoustic cues (Zhang et al., 2025a; Xu et al., 2024). Related work has also explored acoustic-grounded reasoning (Tian et al., 2025), ordinal modeling of affective dimensions (Chandra et al., 2025), and multimodal LLMs for depression assessment (Zhang et al., 2024, 2025b; Zhao et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026). These approaches show that ALMs can gen-erate semantically rich descriptions of vocal be-havior and support flexible affective reasoning. However, free-form descriptions are difficult to compare consistently across utterances and partic-ipants, while closed-set affective classifiers often compress vocal behavior into coarse categories. Existing systems primarily optimize prediction or explanation generation rather than constructing a structured representation that preserves context-dependent paralinguistic distinctions. ParaCalib addresses this gap by mapping con-textualized vocal descriptions into fixed Semanti-cally Calibrated Paralinguistic (SC-Para) dimen-sions. This design combines the contextual sen-sitivity of ALM-generated descriptions with the comparability of a structured state space. Unlike generic acoustic representations, SC-Para explic-itly conditions the interpretation of vocal behavior on utterance-level semantic context and inferred communicative function. Unlike free-form cap-tions or explanations, it supports participant-level aggregation and representation-level analysis. 

3 Methodology 

3.1 Overview 

As illustrated in Figure 2, ParaCalib consists of three stages: ALM-based vocal captioning, PSE-based SC-Para extraction, and attention-based MIL aggregation. Given an utterance-level au-dio segment, the frozen ALM generates a natural-language description that captures both the spo-ken content and the accompanying vocal behav-ior. The frozen PSE then converts this de-scription into a fixed-dimensional SC-Para vec-tor. Finally, the attention-based MIL classifier aggregates utterance-level SC-Para vectors into a participant-level representation for depression-label prediction. The ALM and PSE remain frozen throughout training, and only the downstream MIL classifier is optimized. 

3.2 Utterance-level ALM Captioning 

Let ui denote the i-th utterance-level audio seg-ment. We use a frozen Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B Captioner (Xu et al., 2025) 2 to generate a natural-language vocal description: 

di = ALM (ui). (1) The ALM processes the audio input as a uni-fied audio-language signal. It can infer both what is being said and how it is being said, allowing it to describe vocal behavior in relation to the utter-ance content. The generated description may in-clude observable properties such as pitch variabil-ity, speaking rate, rhythm, pause pattern, vocal en-ergy, and voice quality, together with their inter-pretation in the context of the utterance. The captioner receives only the utterance-level audio; no external text prompt is supplied because the captioning instruction is built into the special-ized checkpoint. The ALM is not fine-tuned on ei-ther dataset and does not receive participant-level depression labels. The checkpoint and decoding settings are provided in Appendix A.3.

4 Data and Evaluation 

4.1 Datasets 

We evaluate ParaCalib on two speech-based de-pression datasets: DAIC-WOZ (Gratch et al., 2014) and MODMA (Cai et al., 2022). DAIC-WOZ contains 189 English-speaking par-ticipants and provides an official participant-disjoint training, development, and test split. We define depression-label status using a PHQ-8 threshold of 10 (Kroenke et al., 2009). Following prior preprocessing (Bailey and Plumbley, 2021), we correct the label assignment of participant 409. The resulting cohort contains 57 depression-label and 132 non-depression-label participants. MODMA contains 52 Mandarin-speaking par-ticipants, with 18 interview utterances per partic-ipant. Although the original participant groups were established through psychiatric assessment, including the MINI under DSM-IV criteria, our experiments use PHQ-9-defined depression labels with a threshold of 10 (Kroenke et al., 2001). Following prior preprocessing (Li et al., 2025a), this criterion reassigns participant 2010037 to the non-depression-label group, resulting in 22 depression-label and 30 non-depression-label par-ticipants. Thus, the MODMA results concern PHQ-9-based labels rather than the original diag-nostic grouping. Label definitions and preprocess-ing details are provided in Appendix A. 

4.2 Evaluation Protocol 

For DAIC-WOZ, we use the official training, de-velopment, and test partitions. For MODMA, we perform participant-level five-fold cross-validation and repeat each fold with the same five random seeds. Within each fold, all preprocessing, model selection, and classifier training are conducted without access to the held-out participants. We report participant-level Macro-F1 as the pri-mary metric, together with sensitivity, specificity, and area under the receiver operating characteris-tic curve (AUC). Unless otherwise stated, thresh-olded predictions use a fixed decision threshold of 0.5. All results are reported as mean and stan-dard deviation (SD) in percentage points. Specifi-cally, DAIC-WOZ results are aggregated over five matched random seeds. For MODMA, metrics are first averaged over the five seeds within each fold, and the reported mean and SD are then computed across the five participant-level fold means. Repro-duced acoustic, end-to-end, and self-supervised baselines, including SpeechFormer, use the same participant-level splits, preprocessing procedure, model-selection protocol, and held-out evaluation protocol (Ma et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2022b; Li et al., 2025a). Implementation and model settings are provided in Appendix A.

5.3 Semantic-Context Control 

We next examine whether semantic context affects PSE interpretation independently of the vocal-behavior description. The controlled analysis holds this description fixed and compares acoustic-only, non-depression-related, and depression-related context conditions. Depression-related context increases the PSE evidence score by 8.6 points relative to non-depression-related context (participant-clustered 95% CI [6 .7, 10 .6] ). The corresponding increase relative to acoustic-only input is 8.7 points, whereas non-depression-related context differs from acoustic-only input by only 0.1 points ( p = .92 ). These results provide direct evidence that semantic calibration is operationalized at the PSE stage: the same caption-level acoustic description yields different depression-related paralinguistic evidence under different semantic contexts. Additional test statistics are provided in Appendix B.3.

5.5 Robustness and Error Analysis 

We conduct additional analyses of speaker identity, extraction stability, acoustic robustness, and pre-diction errors. Additional protocols and results are reported in Appendices C and B. A participant-disjoint speaker-verification probe obtains an AUC of 52.9% for the full seven-dimensional SC-Para representation, compared with 78.7% for WavLM embeddings. This re-sult suggests that SC-Para contains substantially less speaker-identity information than the generic SSL representation. In an exploratory DAIC-WOZ test-set analysis, depression-label partici-pants showed lower attention-weighted SC-Para dispersion than non-depression-label participants (mean difference = −0.0327 ; Appendix C.3). Repeated-decoding analysis yields 94–99% ex-act agreement across SC-Para dimensions. Prompt paraphrase analysis also shows high consistency, with correlations of r ≥ .841 . In contrast, addi-tive acoustic noise degrades performance: mixing background noise at 5 dB SNR reduces Macro-F1 from 71.9% to 62.6%. In a small internal human validation, both raters judged 18 of 20 captions and 17 of 20 constituent-state sets as consistent with perceived vocal evi-dence; details are provided in Appendix C.4 and Table 14. Finally, consistent false positives tend to oc-cur near the PHQ-8 cutoff, with a mean PHQ-8 score of 6.4 compared with 2.6 for reliably clas-sified controls. Consistent false negatives con-tain fewer valid utterances, averaging 66 utterances compared with 119 for reliably classified true pos-itives. These descriptive error patterns are summa-rized in Appendix C.

Rather than treating vocal pat-terns as context-independent markers, ParaCalib interprets them jointly with utterance-level seman-tic information and maps the resulting interpreta-tion into a structured state space. Under a fixed caption-level acoustic description, changing the accompanying semantic context shifts the PSE-derived evidence score, demonstrating context sen-sitivity at the PSE stage. The representation comparisons further clar-ify the role of SC-Para. SC-Para substantially outperforms the evaluated handcrafted and SSL-based representations and achieves higher mean Macro-F1 than direct Caption Embedding on both datasets. These consistent numerical improve-ments indicate that the structured state represen-tation preserves and may refine the task-relevant information contained in free-form captions. Im-portantly, SC-Para also provides a compact and fixed-dimensional representation whose interme-diate paralinguistic dimensions can be compared across utterances and participants. The compo-nent analyses further suggest that categorical vo-cal states and PSE-derived holistic scores provide complementary information. This structured representation enables predic-tions to be inspected at the utterance level be-fore participant-level aggregation. State-level as-sociations, attribute-wise removal, and attention-weighted dispersion offer complementary descrip-tions of the patterns used by the classifier. How-ever, these analyses should not be interpreted as causal feature importance or as direct evidence of clinically observed symptoms. They characterize the behavior of the extracted representation and the downstream classifier under the evaluated datasets and protocols. 

7 Conclusion 

ParaCalib operationalizes semantic calibration by converting contextualized vocal descriptions into a compact and inspectable SC-Para representation for participant-level depression-label classification. It achieves the highest mean Macro-F1 among all evaluated representation strategies on both DAIC-WOZ and MODMA, yielding higher mean per-formance than conventional acoustic, SSL-based, transcript-based, and direct caption-embedding al-ternatives. Controlled analysis further shows that the PSE-derived evidence is sensitive to seman-tic context under a fixed caption-level acoustic de-scription. Together, these findings support struc-tured, context-sensitive, and compositional model-ing of vocal behavior. 

8 Limitations 

We note several considerations for interpreting and extending ParaCalib. First, as with other speech-based approaches, performance depends on record-ing conditions and the amount of available speech. Performance decreases under severe additive noise, and participants with fewer valid utterances pro-vide less evidence for participant-level aggrega-tion. These observations motivate future work on noise-robust extraction and uncertainty-aware pre-diction. Second, our experimental targets are binary la-bels derived from PHQ-8 and PHQ-9 cutoffs. Ac-cordingly, the reported results should be inter-preted as depression-label prediction rather than clinical diagnosis. Further validation on prospec-tively collected data and independently adjudi-cated clinical outcomes would be needed before considering clinical deployment. Third, ParaCalib uses frozen generative mod-els for captioning and state extraction. Although the extraction is stable under repeated decoding and prompt paraphrasing, future work should ex-amine robustness across alternative ALMs, PSEs, recording conditions, and prompting choices. The current unknown -filtering procedure also warrants continued evaluation; its discard rates are reported in Table 13. Fourth, the state-association analysis is ex-ploratory because multiple utterances from the same participant share one label. The displayed 

q values do not account for this within-participant clustering and therefore cannot be interpreted as participant-level confirmatory significance. Aparticipant-clustered or participant-level reanaly-sis is needed to confirm these patterns. Finally, DAIC-WOZ and MODMA are evalu-ated independently. The consistent results across these datasets do not constitute a cross-dataset or cross-lingual transfer evaluation. Moreover, the paired comparisons in Table 4 are based on five matched runs or fold-level means and therefore characterize evaluation-unit variation rather than participant-resampling uncertainty. Larger and more diverse cohorts would enable stronger anal-yses of demographic robustness, generalizability, and real-world screening utility. 

Ethics Statement 

This study follows the ACL Ethics Policy and uses existing speech datasets for computational mental health research. 

Data Access and Original Consent We ob-tained DAIC-WOZ (Gratch et al., 2014) and MODMA (Cai et al., 2022) through their offi-cial distribution channels and used them in ac-cordance with the applicable research-access con-ditions. The original dataset documentation de-scribes the corresponding ethical-approval and informed-consent procedures. Our study involved no new participant recruitment, participant contact, or prospective data collection. 

Privacy and Data Protection Although direct identifiers had been removed by the dataset providers, speech recordings remain sensitive and potentially identifiable biometric data. We there-fore treated the recordings and participant-level la-bels as protected research data. Access was limited to the research workflow, and we made no attempt to identify speakers, recover personal information, or link participants to external records. The ALM and PSE models were run locally; raw audio and participant-level data were not submitted to third-party hosted model services. Generated captions and SC-Para representations were used only for the analyses described in this study. 

Human Validation The small-scale validation described in Appendix C.4 was conducted inter-nally by two graduate researchers. No external an-notators were recruited and no additional compen-sation was provided. Annotators evaluated only whether the generated captions and constituent par-alinguistic states were consistent with perceived vocal evidence; they were not asked to infer par-ticipant identity, life circumstances, mental state, or clinical diagnosis. 

Intended Use and Clinical Interpretation 

ParaCalib is a research framework for studying context-sensitive paralinguistic representations. It is not a medical device and should not be used for diagnosis, triage, treatment decisions, eligibility determinations, or covert monitoring. The predic-tion targets in this study are binary labels derived from PHQ-8 and PHQ-9 thresholds. These labels indicate questionnaire-defined symptom burden and are not equivalent to independently established clinical diagnoses. Any prospective clinical use would require external validation, appropriate regulatory and ethical review, and qualified human oversight. 

Bias and Generalizability Speech-based predic-tion can be affected by language, accent, dialect, age, gender, culture, recording conditions, and ac-cess to care. DAIC-WOZ and MODMA represent limited English- and Mandarin-speaking cohorts and were evaluated independently; the experiments do not establish cross-lingual or cross-population generalizability. ALM-generated descriptions and PSE-derived states may also reflect biases or errors inherited from their pretrained models. Accord-ingly, the extracted states should be interpreted as model-derived representations rather than clini-cally observed symptoms. Evaluation across larger and more diverse populations is necessary before considering broader use.

A.1 Dataset Details and Preprocessing 

We correct two known annotation inconsistencies. For DAIC-WOZ, we relabel participant 409 as de-pressed following Bailey et al. (Bailey and Plumb-ley, 2021). C.3 Attention-Weighted Dispersion Measure 

To assess the internal consistency of participant-level representations, we computed an attention-weighted dispersion measure across valid utterance-level SC-Para vectors. Lower values indicate that the utterances receiving higher attention weights have more similar categorical SC-Para patterns within a participant. Group differences were evaluated using a subject-level permutation test, with a bootstrap confidence interval for the mean difference, a Mann–Whitney 

U test, and Cliff’s delta as an effect-size estimate. In the DAIC-WOZ test split, depression-label participants had lower mean attention-weighted dispersion than non-depression-label participants (0.3617 vs. 0.3945; n = 14 and n = 33 , respec-tively). The mean difference was −0.0327 (boot-strap 95% CI [−0.0593 , −0.0081] ; subject-level permutation p = .0189 ). The result was consis-tent with the Mann–Whitney U test ( p = .0467 ), with Cliff’s δ = −0.3723 . Given the small test-set groups, this analysis is considered exploratory. 

C.4 Human Validation 

Two annotators (graduate researchers proficient in the evaluated languages) evaluated 20 randomly sampled utterances. Since this small-scale valida-tion was conducted internally as part of routine re-search activities, no external recruitment or addi-tional financial compensation was involved. The generated captions and the five PSE-derived con-stituent states were judged consistent with per-ceived vocal evidence in 18/20 and 17/20 samples, respectively (Table 14). 

D Prompts and Output Schema 

Table 15 presents the content-masking prompt, an abbreviated SC-Para feature schema, and the PSE extraction template used in ParaCalib. Prompt 1: Content Masking (Lexical Cue Removal) 

You are an audio description filtering expert. Your task is to filter out speech content transcription from audio descriptions and keep only the audio feature descriptions. ↪→

Content to REMOVE: 1. Specific words and phrases spoken by the speaker (direct quotes) 2. Detailed descriptions of conversation content 3. Explanations of semantic meaning and topics discussed Content to KEEP: 1. Speaker's voice characteristics (timbre, tone, pitch, voice quality) 2. Speaking patterns (pace, rhythm, pauses, cadence) ... Return ONLY the filtered description without any explanations or additional comments. If the entire description is transcription content, return an empty string. ↪→

Prompt 2: SC-Para Feature Definitions & Schema 

Feature definitions: prosody.pitch_variability: - "flat": reduced pitch variation, monotonous or restricted intonation. - "moderate": normal or balanced pitch variation after semantic calibration. - "high": large pitch variation that cannot be explained only by transcript context. - "unknown": insufficient evidence. ... [Other definitions omitted for brevity: speaking_rate, rhythm, pauses, energy] ... depression_related_paralinguistic_evidence.value: - A calibrated depression-related paralinguistic evidence score from 0 to 100. depression_related_paralinguistic_evidence.confidence: - A value from 0.0 to 1.0. Higher confidence means the evidence is clear. 

Prompt 3: PSE Extraction Template 

You are an expert in paralinguistic analysis of depression-related speech patterns. IMPORTANT CONSTRAINTS: - You CANNOT infer identity, mental diagnosis, or life situation. - You are NOT making a medical diagnosis. - Your task is to estimate the strength of depression-related paralinguistic evidence at the utterance level ONLY. ↪→

Input: A caption generated by an audio-language model for a single utterance. Your task: 1. Extract acoustically observable vocal and paralinguistic information. 2. Based on vocal and paralinguistic patterns, estimate the presence and strength of depression-related speech characteristics. ↪→

3. If vocal information is sparse or ambiguous, use "unknown" and reduce confidence. Output STRICTLY in the following JSON format. Each JSON corresponds to ONE utterance. {"prosody": { "pitch_variability": "flat/moderate/high/unknown", ... }, "depression_related_paralinguistic_evidence": { "value": 0-100, "confidence": 0.0-1.0 }}
