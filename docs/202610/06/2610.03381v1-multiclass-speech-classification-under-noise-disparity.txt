MULTICLASS SPEECH CLASSIFICATION UNDER NOISE DISPARITY
Mahdi Amiri1,2, Sayantan Biswas2, Mingchi Hou1,2, Pascal Frossard2, Ina Kodrasi1
1Idiap Research Institute, Switzerland

ABSTRACT
We investigate noise disparity in multi-class speech classification
tasks and develop a strategy to prevent classifiers from exploiting
class-dependent noise characteristics.
Building on our previous
work for binary classification, we introduce a multi-class cross-
augmentation scheme that exposes each class to the noise char-
acteristics of the other classes, thereby removing the association
between individual noise conditions and class labels. We compare
this training-based approach with speech enhancement (SE) as a
preprocessing strategy, which aims to suppress noise-related cues
directly from the input. Experiments on multi-class emotion recog-
nition show that cross-augmentation effectively mitigates the effect
of noise disparity across a range of signal-to-noise-ratios, while SE
has a detrimental effect on model performance.
Index Terms— speech emotion recognition, spurious correla-
tion, speech enhancement, noise augmentation
1. INTRODUCTION
Self-supervised learning (SSL) foundation models have emerged as
powerful general-purpose speech representations, achieving state-
of-the-art performance across a wide range of classification tasks
such as emotion recognition [1,2], pathological speech detection [3–
6], or speaker verification [7, 8]. Models such as wav2vec 2.0 [9]
and XLS-R [10] provide rich speech representations without requir-
ing task-specific feature engineering, and have therefore become in-
creasingly prevalent in downstream speech classification tasks.
However, the strong representational capacity of SSL models
also makes them susceptible to exploiting spurious correlations in
the training data.
A particularly problematic setting arises when
noise characteristics are systematically associated with class labels, a
phenomenon we refer to as noise disparity. Such disparity can natu-
rally arise when recordings from different classes are collected under
different acoustic conditions, with different recording devices or in
different environments. In this situation, noise becomes predictive
of the class label, allowing a classifier to achieve high performance
by exploiting noise-related cues rather than the speech characteris-
tics relevant to the target task. Importantly, this problem can remain
hidden when evaluation data share the same noise characteristics
as the training data, while causing substantial performance degra-
dation when the acoustic conditions change. For example, in [11]
we showed that binary pathological speech classifiers trained on the
TORGO [12] and UASpeech [13] datasets perform considerably bet-
ter on non-speech than on speech segments, demonstrating that they
exploit recording-related characteristics present in non-speech seg-
ments rather than pathology-relevant properties of the speech signal.
This work was supportecd by the Swiss National Science Foundation
projects CRSII5 202228 on “Characterisation of motor speech disorders and
processes” and 200021 215187 on “Pathological Speech Enhancement”.
Similar reliance on non-task-related cues has been observed in other
speech classification tasks [14,15].
An intuitive way to mitigate noise disparity is to suppress the
noise from the recordings before classification. Speech enhance-
ment (SE) models [16, 17] are widely used as preprocessing mod-
ules for downstream speech tasks [18], and therefore provide a nat-
ural candidate for this purpose. In principle, if class-discriminative
noise is sufficiently suppressed, the classifier cannot exploit noise-
related cues. However, SE does not simply remove noise, as en-
hancement models can also modify the speech signal and introduce
artifacts whose characteristics depend on the input noise, signal-to-
noise ratio (SNR), and enhancement model itself [19]. If these arti-
facts vary systematically with the original noise conditions, they can
themselves become predictive of the class labels. Consequently, SE
may replace one source of spurious information with another, rather
than eliminating the underlying shortcut.
An alternative strategy is to instead modify the training data it-
self, removing the association between noise and class label directly.
To suppress noise-related cues for the binary pathological speech
detection task, we previously proposed an augmentation-based ap-
proach that combines each training sample with a proxy of the noise
extracted from the opposite class [20]. This augmentation exposes
both classes to comparable noise characteristics, suppressing the as-
sociation between noise and class labels so that a classifier can only
succeed by relying on genuine speech content. This formulation,
however, is defined for pairs of samples. In the multiclass setting,
a sample can be matched with noise from several other classes, and
it is not clear a priori which noise, or which combination of noises,
should be added.
In this paper, we propose an extension of the binary augmenta-
tion approach from [20] to the multiclass setting, using multiclass
speech emotion recognition as an exemplary task. Additionally, we
investigate whether SE can provide an alternative means of mitigat-
ing noise-related shortcuts. Our main contributions are as follows:
• We demonstrate that noise disparity can induce spurious
class-discriminative cues in multiclass speech classification,
resulting in models that rely on noise characteristics rather
than task-relevant speech information.
• We extend our augmentation strategy [20] from binary to
multiclass classification by comparing three cross-class noise
augmentation schemes. We show that augmenting each sam-
ple with noise from all other classes effectively suppresses
the noise-disparity shortcut across SNR conditions, while
random and batch-pairing strategies are ineffective.
• We systematically investigate SE as a commonly used prepro-
cessing strategy for handling noisy inputs. We show that in
the presence of noise disparity, SE models introduce spurious
enhancement-dependent artifacts that also impair classifica-
tion performance.

4. NOISE DISPARITY IN THE MULTICLASS SETTING
For C > 2 classes, the formulation of Section 3 does not specify
which noise a sample should be augmented with, since the noises of
C −1 other classes are available. The most direct extension is to
augment each sample with the estimated noise of every other class,
so that all augmented samples contain the same C noise sources.
Generalizing (5), the augmented samples have matched noise char-
acteristics if their SNR is the same with respect to each individual
noise source and with respect to any combination of noise sources,
which amounts to 2C −1 conditions. Under the same assumptions
as in [20], these conditions are satisfied by the pairwise coefficients
of (6). However, stacking several noise signals onto a single sample
lowers its effective SNR, with the reduction increasing as the num-
ber of classes grows. We therefore also consider two variants that
randomly introduce noise from only a single class per sample. In
this case, the formulation is equivalent to considering only pairwise
conditions as in the binary classification setting, without a guarantee
of matching all noise characteristics across all classes. In addition,
we evaluate SE as an alternative approach that aims to suppress noise
entirely prior to classification, rather than balancing it across classes.
1. Augmentation with noise from all other classes.
Each noisy
sample is augmented with a scaled noise estimate from every other

2. Random augmentation with a single noise.
At each training
epoch, one noise sample is drawn from a randomly selected differ-
ent class and added using (6). Thus, only one noise component is
introduced at a time, and the SNR does not degrade as the number of
classes increases. In contrast to the previous strategy, this approach
does not satisfy all possible conditions in the multiclass setting and


therefore does not guarantee effective suppression of noise disparity.
3. Pairing samples in each batch. Within each training batch, sam-
ples from different classes are randomly paired and cross-augmented
via (4), which applies the exact symmetric two-class matching con-
dition rather than the random matching used in the previous strategy.
Samples that cannot be paired within a batch are instead assigned
noise from a randomly chosen sample of another class. We consider
this variant in addition to strategy 2 to test whether enforcing an
exact mutual noise match for at least some class pairs per batch is
more effective than the weaker additive exposure in strategy 2.
4. Using speech enhancement as a preprocessing step.
An alterna-
tive approach to mitigating noise disparity is to suppress noise be-
fore training. To the best of our knowledge, the effectiveness of SE
as a preprocessing step for this purpose has not been systematically
studied. We therefore apply an enhancement model to the noisy sam-
ples prior to training the model, aiming to reduce class-specific noise
cues. Specifically, we consider a complex-valued spectrogram-based
regression model (CR) [16] and a Schr¨odinger Bridge-based model
(SB) [22], representing discriminative and generative state-of-the-art
SE paradigms. Their implementation and training configurations are
detailed in Section 5.2.
5. EXPERIMENTAL SETTINGS
5.1. Databases
To investigate noise disparity in a controlled setting, we require a
dataset in which the association between class labels and noise char-
acteristics can be explicitly controlled. To this end, we follow the
procedure of [20] and construct a synthetic noisy dataset by adding
selected environmental noises to clean speech samples. This allows
us to introduce noise disparity while keeping the underlying speech
signal unchanged.
Clean dataset.
For the clean dataset, we use the IEMOCAP
dataset [23] which is a widely used benchmark for multiclass emo-
tion recognition. IEMOCAP comprises five sessions, each featur-
ing one male and one female speaker. Although the original dataset
contains ten emotion classes, we follow the commonly used four-
class protocol, considering the angry, neutral, sad, and happy classes
(with excited merged into happy) [1,2]. These four classes comprise
5, 531 samples with a total duration of approximately 7 hours.
Noise dataset.
For generating the noisy samples, we use
the DEMAND noise dataset [24].
Specifically, we select the
DKITCHEN, NPARK, OMEETING, and PCAFETER noises. Each
noise type is assigned to exactly one emotion class, such that the
noise characteristics are systematically associated with the class la-
bels. We consider three scenarios corresponding to high, moderate,
and random SNRs:
A. Samples from all classes are corrupted with their respective
noises at an SNR of 20 dB.
B. Samples from all classes are corrupted with their respective
noises at an SNR of 10 dB.
C. Samples from all classes are corrupted with their respective
noises at an SNR randomly chosen from the range of 10 to
20 dB.
5.2. Speech enhancement models
We consider two state-of-the-art SE models, the discriminative CR
model from [16] and the generative SB model from [17].
Both
SE models follow a U-Net architecture. We took the best check-
point trained on the widely used SE benchmark dataset WSJ0-
CHiME3 [25]. This benchmark is composed of clean speech from
the English Wall Street Journal dataset [26] and noise from the
ChiME3 dataset [27].
5.3. Training Protocol
For conducting our experiments, we use a wav2vec2-based [9] ap-
proach. We obtain the feature representation from the raw audio sig-
nal using the last layer of the wav2vec 2.0 base model. We then pass
these representations through two linear layers, which project the
representation into 256 and 2 dimensions, respectively. We also use
a ReLU nonlinearity between the linear layers. We segment signals
into 5-second chunks with 50% overlap. The final residual segment
of each utterance is also retained.
Training is done using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate
of 1 × 10−3. We also use ReduceLROnPlateau with a patience of
5 and a factor of 0.5. Models are trained until a maximum number
of 100 epochs or until the learning rate decreases below 10−4 of the
initial learning rate. The best model is chosen based on the loss value
on the validation set.
Training and evaluation is done through the commonly used
leave-one-session-out cross-validation setting [1]. At each fold, we
use one of the sessions as the test set, and the remaining four ses-
sions are split into a training set and a validation set with a ratio of
0.8 and 0.2, respectively. Performance is evaluated using the Un-
weighted Accuracy (UA), which is commonly used for evaluating
emotion recognition systems with imbalanced emotion classes [1].
6. EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS
In this section, we compare the effectiveness of different training
strategies for mitigating the effects of noise disparity. As a base-
line, we consider standard training in which the model is trained
directly on the noisy training data without any augmentation or pre-
processing. This baseline is compared with the proposed augmen-
tation strategies. In addition, we investigate the SE-based training
strategy where the noisy training data are first enhanced using ei-
ther the CR or SB model and the classifier is subsequently trained
on the enhanced data. Furthermore, we train and test the model on
clean data to obtain a sense of its performance when there is no noise
disparity in the dataset. The obtained UA in this case is 59.3%.
For the augmentation-based approaches described in Section 4,
augmenting each signal with noise from another class according
to (7) requires an estimate of the corresponding noise signal. To in-
vestigate the effect of potential errors in the estimated noise signals,
we consider two settings:
1. Oracle.
Since we generate the synthetic noisy dataset our-
selves, the exact noise signal in each sample is known. In the
oracle setting, we use this ground-truth noise signal during
augmentation. This setting allows us to evaluate the augmen-
tation strategy without errors in the noise estimate.
2. Practical.
In practice, the noise signal is unknown and must
be estimated from the noisy speech. Following [20], we use
the VAD from [21] to separate speech and non-speech seg-
ments of the noisy samples. Assuming that the non-speech
segments contain no speech information, we use them as an
estimate of the background noise for augmentation. Errors
in this estimate therefore reflect the noise estimation errors
encountered in a practical setting.

All models are evaluated on two common test sets, i.e., the orig-
inal noisy test set, which exhibits the same class-dependent noise
as the training data, and the corresponding clean test set. A model
that relies only on speech content cannot have a higher accuracy on
the noisy test set than on the clean one. A higher accuracy on the
noisy test set therefore indicates reliance on class-dependent noise
cues, and the gap between the two serves as our main measure of
spurious-cue exploitation. Since SE-based models are trained on en-
hanced data, and would also receive enhanced inputs at deployment,
we additionally evaluate them on the noisy test set enhanced with
the SB and the CR model. The clean-data reference (UA of 59.3%)
indicates what can be achieved from speech content alone. Accuracy
on enhanced data exceeding it therefore indicates that the classifier
exploits cues introduced or preserved by SE rather than task-related
information. Results for the augmentation strategies are given in Ta-
ble 1, and for the SE-based strategies in Table 2.
As can be seen in Table 1, when the model is trained under stan-
dard training, its performance on the noisy test set is much higher
than on the clean test set across all SNR settings. This confirms
that the model learns noise-discriminant cues rather than actual task-
related cues. When augmenting each sample with oracle noise from
every other class, our proposed method can effectively close the per-
formance gap between the noisy and clean test sets. This shows that
given a precise estimate of the noise, our proposed method can make
training robust to spurious correlations. Under the practical setup
in the SNR setting of 20 dB, we also successfully close the gap be-
tween the noisy and clean test sets, though this comes at the cost of
lower overall performance compared to the oracle setup. Under the
practical setup in the SNR settings of 10 dB and random, the gap
between noisy and clean test set performance is reduced by a large
margin but does not fully close. We argue that with a better estimate
of the noise, this remaining gap could be closed to match that of the
oracle scenario.
In the training strategy in which noise from another class is ran-
domly selected for each sample, clean set accuracy increases and
noisy set accuracy decreases compared with standard training. How-
ever, this strategy is overall not effective, with the performance gap
between noisy and clean test sets remaining large. This trend can
also be observed in the strategy where we pair samples within each
batch and cross-augment their noise. We argue this occurs because
the noise is changed for each sample at every training epoch, encour-
aging the model to become invariant to the augmented noise and in-
stead focus on the components that remain fixed across epochs. As a
result, the model can still exploit the original class-dependent noise
characteristics, leading to a persistent performance gap between the
noisy and clean test sets.
The enhancement-based strategies in Table 2 show a different
behavior. While training with either CR or SB results in low per-
formance on the noisy and clean test sets, performance is substan-
tially higher on the enhanced test sets, particularly when the same
enhancement model is used for training and testing. Notably, per-
formance on the enhanced test sets is also substantially higher than
when training and testing on the clean data (i.e., 59.3%). Since the
clean data contain only the task-relevant speech information in our
controlled setup, the additional discriminative information in the en-
hanced data must originate from the enhancement of the noisy sig-
nal. Thus, rather than removing the class-dependent noise cues, SE
appears to preserve or introduce noise-dependent artifact cues that
can be exploited by the classifier. Consequently, SE does not pro-
vide an effective solution for suppressing noise disparity.
In summary, experimental results show that augmenting each
sample with the noise of every other class is the only strategy that
removes the reliance on class-dependent noise cues in multiclass set-
tings. In contrast, single-class augmentation leaves a substantial gap
between noisy and clean test set performance, while SE introduces
new spurious cues rather than removing existing ones.
7. CONCLUSION
We investigated noise disparity between classes in multiclass speech
classification, using a synthetic dataset built by introducing con-
trolled noise disparity into the IEMOCAP corpus [23]. Evaluating
this setup across several SNR conditions and test sets, we showed
that noise disparity leads models to learn spurious, noise-based cues
rather than task-relevant information. To address this, we proposed
and compared three cross-augmentation strategies, finding that aug-
menting each sample with noise from every other class provides the
strongest robustness. We further showed that speech enhancement
applied as a preprocessing step does not resolve this issue, but in-
stead introduces its own noise- and enhancement-specific artifacts.
